\begin{abstract}
Long-form text-to-speech must retain requested words over extended generations,
yet sentence-level training and evaluation can hide omissions and early stops.
We introduce \corpus{}, a Russian corpus of 189 hours in continuous units of
30 seconds to 15 minutes. Paired long sequences and short windows support
fine-tuning comparisons on the same source recordings. The accompanying
\dataset{} evaluation retains every synthesis attempt. Alongside an earlier
controlled CosyVoice3 experiment, a separate completed evaluation uses 50 published-text
pairs from 22 works and 21 held-out voices across three autoregressive backbones
and F5-TTS flow matching. At approximately 1,200 words, CosyVoice3's Long-minus-Short
WER change is $-52.7$ percentage points.
For the other backbones, paired intervals favor Long-SFT-Punct for Qwen3-TTS, VoxCPM2 and F5-TTS. These results characterize the
effect of preserving training sequence length under a fixed continuous-generation
protocol; they do not establish listener-rated quality or superiority to chunked synthesis.

\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
text-to-speech, Russian speech, speech corpus, long-form synthesis
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Audiobooks, lectures and podcasts require speech synthesis to preserve content
and voice beyond a sentence. Short outputs can conceal omissions, repetitions
and early stops, and listeners judge sentences and paragraphs differently
\cite{clark2019evaluating}. Existing pipelines can obtain long audio by splitting
text into short generations~\cite{qwen2025cosyvoicecode,chen2026f5code}. Studying
one uninterrupted generation requires data and evaluation that retain longer context.

We introduce \corpus{} for this purpose in Russian. The Balalaika
pipeline~\cite{borodin2026balalaika} supplies context-preserving segmentation,
transcripts, provenance and quality screening; the corpus retains continuous
speech units up to 15 minutes. Paired short-window and long-sequence training
views allow a source-controlled comparison of sequence length, with channel-disjoint
train, development and test splits.

We evaluate these views on CosyVoice3, Qwen3-TTS, VoxCPM2 and F5-TTS. The earlier
controlled CosyVoice3 experiment tests duration under closely matched training
exposure (Table~\ref{tab:bybucket}). A separate published-text comparison tests
50 fixed text--voice pairs from 22 works (Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel}), retaining
every failure in word-error and correct-word-recall estimates. Paired Short/Long
comparisons quantify the effect and its uncertainty for all four backbones.
The paired intervals favor long targets across the four tested backbones.

Our contributions are the long-form corpus, paired training views and an
all-attempt evaluation of corpus use across generation families. The experiments
separate this data question from the choice to generate continuously or in chunks.
Transcript-source and formatting changes, and VoxCPM2's retained-audio differences,
remain explicit confounds rather than isolated duration effects.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Speech corpora for synthesis.}
Open TTS corpora make data quality and coverage reproducible research choices.
LibriTTS-R improves the acoustic quality of an existing English corpus through speech
restoration~\cite{koizumi2023librittsr}, while HiFiTTS-2 scales audiobook-derived data
with quality filtering and detailed metadata~\cite{langman2025hifitts2}.
For Russian, RUSLAN provides carefully recorded single-speaker
speech~\cite{gabdrakhmanov2019ruslan}, and Balalaika provides a pipeline for
context-preserving segmentation, multi-ASR transcription, and speaker-purity
screening~\cite{borodin2026balalaika}.  We build on the latter to produce a corpus
organized around long continuous speech units.  Its paired short and long training
views let us examine sequence duration while keeping the source recordings fixed.

\noindent\textbf{Approaches to long-form generation.}
Recent work addresses long speech through inference and model design.
MagpieTTS-LF carries state and text history across sentence chunks without
retraining~\cite{ghosh2026magpiettslf}.  VoiceStar combines positional modeling and a
training scheme for duration control and extrapolation~\cite{peng2026voicestar}.
F5-TTS offers a non-autoregressive flow-matching
alternative~\cite{chen2024f5tts}; its standard long-text inference splits the text
and joins separately synthesized chunks~\cite{chen2026f5code}.
These approaches show that long-form behavior can be improved through several
mechanisms.  Our study examines the contribution of training data: whether preserving
long targets helps existing models under a fixed uninterrupted-inference protocol.
It does not assume that long-form training is necessary for every synthesis method.

\noindent\textbf{Backbones and evaluation.}
CosyVoice~\cite{du2024cosyvoice2,du2025cosyvoice3},
Qwen3-TTS~\cite{hu2026qwen3tts}, and VoxCPM2~\cite{zhou2026voxcpm2}
provide open autoregressive models with different speech representations and generation behavior;
Qwen3-TTS also reports a dedicated long-speech evaluation.  We use their public
fine-tuning paths to assess the same corpus across backbones.
LFSBench evaluates long-form speech across scenarios and dimensions including
content, acoustics, and expressiveness~\cite{pan2026lfsbench}.
Our benchmark addresses a narrower training-data question: it controls input
duration, retains failed attempts in the reported metrics, and evaluates whether a
single generation reads the requested text.  Speaker similarity and quality proxies
are reported separately because completion alone does not establish perceptual quality.

\section{Corpus and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Corpus, data splits, and training arms}

\corpus{} is released openly on \datasetlink{}.

The open-source Balalaika pipeline~\cite{borodin2026balalaika} provided context-aware
segmentation, multi-ASR ROVER transcription, and quality and speaker-purity screening.
DeepFilterNet3~\cite{schroter2023deepfilternet} attenuated applause and background noise
on working copies; originals were retained unchanged and only enhanced derivatives
entered training.

\corpus{} v3.1 contains 6,380 Creative-Commons Russian segments (189.13 h; 30--900 s,
single speaker) from 313 videos and 41 channels, with text from recognizer-output voting
(ROVER)~\cite{fiscus1997rover} over available ASR hypotheses.  We partition by YouTube
channel before windowing: all segments of a channel belong to one split
(Table~\ref{tab:splits}).  All SFT uses train only; dev supplies validation and the two-voice evaluation's
text selection.  Channel identity is a conservative document/speaker proxy, supplemented
by exact-text and 8-gram leakage checks and a speaker-embedding gate across splits.

Training starts from 6,043 long units or 28,532 short windows of the same recordings
(170.68 h, 1,257,944 words before empty-text exclusions).  For \model{}, each long speech-token
sequence concatenates its windows.  Short-SFT and
Long-SFT use ROVER text; Long-SFT-Punct keeps the Long-SFT audio tokens but substitutes
punctuated, capitalized GigaAM-v3-e2e-CTC transcripts.  This also changes words: after
the same evaluation normalization, 95.0\% of the 6,041 training transcript pairs differ,
with 5.4\% pooled word-edit disagreement relative to ROVER.  This is not a
punctuation-only ablation.

\subsection{Fine-tuning to assess corpus utility}
\label{sec:sft}

The main comparison changes training-sequence length while controlling exposure to
the corpus.  All \model{} arms start from the same checkpoint and fine-tune only the
language model; the speech tokenizer, acoustic generator, and speaker encoder stay
fixed.  They use 3,000 updates with the same learning rate and loss normalization.
Relative to Long-SFT, observed target-token exposure differs by 1.4\% for Short-SFT
and by 1.27\% for Long-SFT-Punct.

We fine-tune Qwen3-TTS and VoxCPM2 with their public training
code~\cite{qwen2026qwen3ttscode,openbmb2026voxcpmcode}, using punctuated text in both
the short and long arms.  Within-backbone exposure differs by 0.17\% for Qwen3-TTS
and 0.3\% for VoxCPM2.  VoxCPM2's context-length filter excludes 144 long units
lasting 517--899 s, or 18.2\% of the audio, leaving units of at most 661 s.
The short arm retains windows from these recordings, so its comparison does not
fully isolate duration from corpus composition.  The \model{} and Qwen3-TTS arms
share recording sets.

For F5-TTS, both arms fine-tune the full Russian v1 Base model with the flow-matching
objective and punctuated transcripts.  They traverse the same 6,038 recordings in
the same order for three passes (18,114 updates), using intact recordings or short
windows.  Long/Short consume 510.92/510.26 hours of training audio, within 0.24\% of
the corresponding Qwen replication budgets; final exponential-moving-average (EMA) weights are evaluated without
score-based checkpoint selection.  Both arms exclude records lacking usable windows;
residual normalized transcript disagreement is 0.04\%.  Full training and runtime
settings accompany the code.

\subsection{State-continuous inference and evaluation sets}

Continuous inference uses one target text and one voice-conditioning event,
followed by an unsplit autoregressive trajectory or full-sequence flow solve.
External splitting, state resets, best-of-$N$ selection and regeneration are
disabled; all attempts are retained. The CosyVoice3 text splitter is bypassed.
Operational context, output and acoustic-buffer limits are fixed across
checkpoints within each backbone and documented with the code. F5-TTS estimates
duration from prompt audio and text length; it has no EOS decision, and planned
duration is not a learned stopping measure.

The earlier two-voice controlled set combines six source texts (four development
recordings and two constructed stress texts), five nested duration ranges and
two held-out voices: 60 attempts per arm (Table~\ref{tab:bybucket}). The new
published-text set uses 50 pairs from 22 works and 21 reference speakers held
out from fine-tuning: eight new external readers and 13 used in the earlier
evaluation. Each work supplies one long passage near 1,200 normalized words;
14 short and 14 medium prefixes target 75 and 300 words. Length ranges are
60--90, 240--360 and 960--1,440 words, rather than reference-audio durations.
Inputs are literal, sentence-complete published spans, screened against local
training and prior evaluation text. The same fixed pairs are used by every arm.

A submission-deadline reduction from 120 to 50 pairs followed inspection of
preliminary Base/Short results and Long-run progress. The deterministic selector
used only source and speaker metadata, without scores or output duration;
this scope amendment is not a pre-synthesis selection. The 120-pair study is
deferred. The completed comparison includes all 13 continuous conditions.
All input is punctuated and capitalized. Among the CosyVoice3 arms, only
Long-SFT-Punct matches this format in training; the two adapted arms of each
other backbone are format-matched.

\subsection{Evaluator and paired uncertainty}
\label{sec:evaluator}

GigaAM-v3-RNNT~\cite{kutsakov2025gigaam} transcribes every generated output.
Mean per-attempt WER counts substitutions, deletions and insertions relative to
reference words; correct-word recall is aligned correct words divided by
reference words. Unusable audio becomes an empty hypothesis. Failed and empty
outputs remain in every denominator, and WER is not clipped at 100\%.
The published-text set has no paired human recording, so no human-ASR floor or
floor-dependent completion label is used for its quality results.

Table~\ref{tab:bybucket} retains the earlier completion rule, frozen before SFT:
an autoregressive EOS before 95\% aligned text coverage is early; remaining
outputs are degraded by WER at least 30 points above a human-ASR floor or fixed
repetition criteria. Watchdog stops retain their label. Constructed texts lack
human recordings and skip the WER-floor test, so complete does not imply a
faithful transcript. Earlier F5 missing-tail events are incomplete text, never EOS.

For the new set, each backbone's primary contrast compares its long and short
arms (Long-SFT for CosyVoice3, Long-SFT-Punct for the other backbones)
on the 22 longest inputs. Paired pointwise 95\% intervals use 10,000 resamples
of the 22 work clusters, retaining associated prefixes and voices; independently
resampling work and voice levels is a crossed-factor sensitivity check.
Intervals condition on fitted checkpoints and inference seed, omit retraining
variation, and do not adjust for multiple comparisons. Earlier two-voice intervals
resample its six source texts separately.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Earlier controlled duration comparison}
\label{sec:duration}

Long supervision improves native \model{} reading with two reference voices
(Table~\ref{tab:bybucket}).
Short-SFT resembles Base, whereas Long-SFT gains 45.00 completion points
[33.33, 56.67] over Short-SFT under matched exposure.  Neither Base nor Short-SFT
completes an item beyond 90 s. The sentence-split control completes all items
at 3.84\% WER; our continuous protocol claims no superiority over it.  Repeated
decoding shifts individual bucket percentages by up to $\sim$20 points, so these
single-seed rows are descriptive.

Punctuated transcripts add a secondary gain. Long-SFT-Punct reaches 83.3\%
completion; the prespecified pooled analysis gives $+10.62$ completion points
[4.55, 16.38] and $-6.69$ WER points over Long-SFT.  This comparison changes both
transcript source and formatting.  Giving Long-SFT lowercased, unpunctuated input
matched to its training format yields 88.3\% completion and 10.07\% WER;
Long-SFT-Punct does not improve significantly on that control.  The gain is therefore
consistent with format matching, while a separate benefit of punctuation content
remains unestablished: this control uses only the two-voice set and one seed, and training
and input formats were not fully crossed.  Three preregistered mixtures with
external short clips did not improve Long-SFT and increased repetition.

\subsection{Content fidelity across backbones}
\label{sec:crossmodel}

The completed published-text comparison tests the training views on new source
passages across four backbones (Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel}). For the three
autoregressive models, Long-minus-Short WER changes at approximately 1,200 words
are CosyVoice3: $-52.7$ pp [-62.7, -42.2]; Qwen3-TTS: $-52.7$ pp [-61.7, -43.4]; VoxCPM2: $-41.5$ pp [-47.7, -35.4].
Negative changes indicate fewer word errors. All are paired effects on the same
22 work passages; short and medium conditions in Fig.~\ref{fig:werlength} show
how absolute error changes with input length. These results do not treat the
50 dependent attempts as 50 independent source passages.

Correct-word recall complements WER by measuring how much reference content is
actually recognized, without counting substitutions as correct. The table retains
poor and failed readings rather than conditioning on completion. CosyVoice3's
punctuated-transcript arm is a separate format/source comparison, while
VoxCPM2's Long/Short comparison also changes retained corpus composition.

\subsection{Flow matching and inference protocol}
\label{sec:f5}

F5-TTS extends the paired comparison to a flow-matching model. Its longest-input
Long-minus-Short WER change is $-4.6$ pp [-4.9, -4.4].
Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel} also reports absolute WER and correct-word recall:
a relative change alone does not establish reliable long-text reading. The
duration schedule is shared across checkpoints, so this comparison evaluates
content realization rather than learning an EOS decision.

Chunking provides a distinct practical reference. On the \emph{earlier} recorded-text
53-pair set, F5 Base WER was 3.58\% with sentence chunking and
73.35\% with continuous synthesis. Those values use different
texts from Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel} and are not rows of the new comparison.
Chunked controls are not included in the completed new-set group; the earlier result establishes no new-set ranking.

\subsection{Evaluation checks and practical limits}

Earlier secondary-ASR checks supported the CosyVoice3 ranking on recorded texts;
the new-set WER uses one RNNT recognizer.
Figure~\ref{fig:qualitytime} adds windowed DistillMOS~\cite{stahl2025distillation}
and WeSpeaker~\cite{wang2022wespeaker} measurements on the same new outputs.
F5 Long-SFT-Punct has higher DistillMOS than Base despite high WER; Qwen3-TTS and
VoxCPM2 still show speaker drift. Curves condition on scorable windows, with
support shown below. Truncated outputs can score well, so these proxies
complement all-attempt WER and do not replace listening tests.

An earlier 5--10-minute CosyVoice3 audit found continuous generation used
$3.67\times$ the wall time and $3.56\times$ the peak memory of sentence splitting;
these costs are protocol-specific.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The published-text set covers 22 selected works and repeated reference voices,
not all Russian speech. Its deadline-driven scope amendment followed inspection
of earlier run summaries, even though the subset selector ignored outcomes.
The 120-pair evaluation and sealed 40-text benchmark remain incomplete.
Earlier CosyVoice3 checkpoint selection and controlled evaluation share development
data. Each backbone has one training seed per arm; paired intervals do not include
retraining, repeated-decoding or selection uncertainty.

GigaAM contributes training transcripts and evaluates outputs, leaving possible
recognizer bias. No listening study establishes naturalness or speaker preference.
Unknown base-model pretraining overlap is not ruled out by local text screening.
Punctuation and transcript-source effects are not fully isolated. VoxCPM2's long
arm retains 81.8\% of the audio, so matched exposure cannot remove the composition
confound; earlier speaker drift also remains unresolved. One F5 model does not
establish architectural causes or generality to all non-autoregressive models.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\corpus{} preserves long continuous Russian speech with paired short-window
training views. The controlled CosyVoice3 study and the completed published-text
comparison provide separate evidence about sequence length. Across CosyVoice3,
Qwen3-TTS, VoxCPM2 and F5-TTS, the study reports absolute content fidelity and
paired Short/Long changes. The paired intervals favor long targets across the four tested backbones.
The corpus supports studying sustained reading while keeping content fidelity
and perceptual quality distinct.
